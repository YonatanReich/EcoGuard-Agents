# Operational Risk Management and Rules of Engagement for Structure Fires

Source: U.S. Fire Administration, *Risk Management Practices in the Fire Service*
(FEMA / USFA). Status: work of the U.S. federal government, public domain.

This is the **structural** counterpart to the wildland documents in this corpus. Where
the NWCG material governs engagement on a fireline and the USFA triage material governs
which buildings to defend from an approaching wildfire, this document governs the
decision to commit crews **into** a structure — the offensive/defensive call, who owns
it, and the conditions under which it must be revisited.

It is the authority to consult for a fire in a building rather than in vegetation:
apartment fires, commercial premises, and any incident where the question is whether to
send people inside.

## The Three Acceptable-Risk Guidelines

There is always some degree of risk involved in conducting emergency operations. The
Incident Commander has to determine the limits of risk that are acceptable for each
situation and direct operations to ensure that those limits are not exceeded.

Three guidelines define acceptable levels of risk:

1. Activities that present a significant risk to the safety of members shall be limited
   to situations in which there is a potential to save endangered lives.
2. Activities routinely employed to protect property shall be recognized as inherent
   risks to the safety of members, and actions shall be taken to reduce or avoid those
   risks.
3. No risk to the safety of members shall be acceptable when there is no possibility to
   save lives or property.

These are ordered deliberately. Life safety justifies significant risk; property alone
justifies only measured risk; and where neither can be saved, no risk is acceptable. A
plan that commits crews to a building with nothing savable inside has failed the third
guideline regardless of how well it is otherwise constructed.

## Rules of Engagement: Offensive or Defensive

The decision to operate offensively (inside, direct attack) or defensively (outside,
containment and exposure protection) is made by cross-referencing the risk to
firefighters against the probability of a successful outcome.

| Firefighter injury / life safety risk | High probability of success | Marginal probability of success | Low probability of success |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Low** | Initiate offensive operations. Continue to monitor risk factors. | Initiate offensive operations. Continue to monitor risk factors. | Initiate offensive operations. Continue to monitor risk factors. |
| **Medium** | Initiate offensive operations. Continue to monitor risk factors. Employ all available risk control options. | Initiate offensive operations. Continue to monitor risk factors. Be prepared to go defensive if risk increases. | Do not initiate offensive operations. Reduce risk to fire fighters and actively pursue risk control options. |
| **High** | Initiate offensive operations only with confirmation of realistic potential to save endangered lives. | Do not initiate offensive operations that will put fire fighters at risk for injury or fatality. | Initiate defensive operations only. |

Read the bottom-right corner first. High risk with low probability of success permits
**defensive operations only** — there is no version of that situation in which sending
crews inside is correct. The top-right corner is equally important and more often
misread: low risk permits an offensive attack even when success is unlikely, because
little is being staked on it.

At high firefighter risk, an offensive attack requires **confirmation of realistic
potential to save endangered lives** — not a suspicion, and not an assumption that the
building is occupied.

## Known Versus Assumed Risk Factors

It is often impossible to obtain and verify all the pertinent information before making
important strategic decisions. The decisions that guide initial actions must often be
made with a limited amount of information, making allowances for factors that are not
known. A rapid size-up seldom allows for a full evaluation of all the risk factors.

An Incident Commander must consciously differentiate between factors that are **known**
and those that are based on **assumptions**, experience and standard approaches. The IC
must then place a priority on either confirming these assumptions or revising them as
soon as factual information can be obtained.

This distinction is doctrine, not caution. A plan that presents an assumption as an
established fact removes the commander's ability to prioritise confirming it. Anything
taken on assumption should be recorded as such, together with what would confirm it.

## Continuous Reassessment

A risk assessment should be reprocessed with every observation and progress report.
Every bit of new information that comes to the Incident Commander should be considered
to see if strategy or tactics should be changed.

Every individual in the incident management structure is responsible for monitoring and
evaluating risks and for keeping the IC informed of any factor that could be a reason to
reconsider the risk management balance.

A response plan is therefore never final. It should name the observations that would
trigger a change of strategy, so that a crew arriving on scene knows what it is watching
for rather than merely what it is doing.

## Roles and Responsibilities

| Role | Responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| **Incident Commander** | Makes strategic decisions based on risk acceptance or avoidance. Those decisions are expressed in the selection of offensive or defensive operations — a major component of the strategic plan for the incident. |
| **Sector and Company Officers** | Supervise tactical operations based on risk acceptance and avoidance within the areas they supervise. Their determinations must be consistent with the direction provided by the Incident Commander, and they must keep the IC informed of anything that may affect the strategic plan. |
| **Individual emergency responders** | Where no officer is present, may be called on to make personal decisions about risk acceptance or avoidance, which must also be consistent with the strategic plan. |

An Incident Commander is responsible for determining the operational mode — offensive or
defensive — for the entire duration of the incident. After making an initial strategic
determination, the IC should define tactical objectives, assign resources to perform
specific functions, and establish a command structure to supervise the operation.

**Whenever command of an incident is transferred, the responsibility for strategic risk
management is transferred with it.**

## Unoccupied, Vacant and Abandoned Structures

The distinguishing characteristics of unoccupied, vacant and abandoned structures
influence an Incident Commander's decisions. The fundamental risk management guidelines
state that the lives of firefighters should not be risked where there is no possibility
of saving lives or property. That restricts entry into many vacant or abandoned
structures for interior operations, on the basis that there are no occupants and there
is nothing of sufficient value to justify the risk.

Occupancy status is therefore a first-order input to the offensive/defensive decision,
not a detail. Where occupancy is unknown, it is an assumption under the section above
and should be treated as one.

## Specialist Capability as a Constraint

Where an incident requires skills or equipment the crews on scene do not have, the
operation must wait until a specialised team responds. The first-arriving officer is
responsible for determining whether personnel at the scene have the skills and equipment
needed to initiate the operation at all.

A plan that assigns an action to a unit not equipped to perform it is not a plan. Where
the required capability is not present, the correct instruction names the specialist
resource and the interim holding action.

## Operational Use in Assessment and Planning

When assessing a fire in a structure, this document supports the following judgements:

- Whether committing crews is permitted at all, via the three acceptable-risk guidelines
  and the offensive/defensive matrix.
- Whether occupancy is known or assumed, and what would confirm it.
- Which observations should trigger a strategy change, and who is responsible for
  reporting them.
- Whether the responding units possess the capability the plan assigns them.

Note what this document does **not** cover: it is a risk-management and command
framework, not a tactical manual. It contains nothing on ventilation, standpipe
operations, ladder placement, search patterns, or fire-flow calculation. A plan
requiring those should say that the guidance is not available here rather than inventing
it.
