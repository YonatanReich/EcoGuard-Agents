# Structure Triage and Resource Allocation in the Wildland/Urban Interface

Source: Brown, Keith. "Structure Triage During Wildland/Urban Interface/Intermix Fires."
Applied research project, National Fire Academy Executive Fire Officer Program, U.S.
Fire Administration, February 1994.
Status: work prepared for a U.S. federal agency programme; public domain.

This is the response-action authority in this corpus. It covers how to decide which
structures to defend when resources cannot protect everything, and when defending a
structure is unsafe and must not be attempted.

## The Scarce-Resource Problem

There are never enough resources to protect every threatened structure; hard decisions
must be made as to when and where intervention will occur. Structure triage is the
process of prioritising structure protection based on perceived defensibility so that
limited resources produce the greatest benefit.

Overly optimistic predictions too often have resulted in unsuccessful attempts to save a
few structures when write-offs would have given suppression forces time to gather in
strength further in advance of the fire, where firefighting efforts would have more
likely stemmed the wildland fire's advance and therefore eliminated the need to defend
individual structures.

The operational implication is that declining to defend a structure is a legitimate and
sometimes superior tactical choice, not a failure.

## Firefighter Safety Governs the Decision

Firefighter safety must always be the first consideration.

The principal factor jeopardising firefighter safety while attempting to defend
structures in wildland fires is **impeded or obstructed egress**. Standard wildland
firefighting orders require that firefighters have at least one, and preferably two,
reliable escape routes at all times.

Narrow driveways with fuel-canopy overhangs, or with proximate accumulations of heavy or
down-dead fuels, contraindicate attack or active defensive efforts by emergency
responders. The narrower the driveway, the greater the threat from fuel-canopy
overhangs.

NFPA 299 requires driveways to be 12 feet wide in the clear with a minimum vertical
unobstructed clearance of 15 feet. Driveways below that standard should be treated as an
access constraint on any engine committed to that structure.

Access assessment covers dead-ends, length, width, slope, grade, surface, and the
presence of turnarounds. A structure at the end of a long, narrow, dead-end driveway with
no turnaround is a candidate for write-off regardless of its other characteristics.

## The Three Principal Survivability Predictors

A small number of factors can be used to accurately predict structure survivability
during wildland fires. In order of importance:

### 1. Roof composition

The foremost predictor of structure survivability is the composition of the roof. The
roof is the most vulnerable part of the house in a fire, and noncombustible roof
coverings are a must.

If a roof is starting to burn, the structure is probably not salvageable. However,
structures already on fire may be saved if the fire is limited to isolated rooms, decks,
eaves, or siding and attack lines are quickly deployed.

### 2. Defensible space

The second most important predictor is the presence or absence of adequate defensible
space. The minimum radius of defensible space should be **30 feet**. Defensible space
should be expanded to compensate for steeper slopes.

Fuel stored within the defensible space — firewood, LPG tanks, and similar — degrades it
and creates additional hazards for crews working near the structure.

### 3. Slope and terrain

The third most significant predictor is a combination of slope and terrain. Steep slopes
are those exceeding **20 percent** (ratio of rise to run); extreme slopes are those
exceeding **40 percent**. Statistics based on case studies predict an unsuccessful
outcome for structure defence when slopes surrounding the structure exceed 20 percent.

Structures located in saddles, at the top of steep slopes, on ridges, and at the top of
ravines are exposed to increased hazard from fire. Terrain also affects the difficulty
of stretching and manoeuvring hoselines. Even structures located at the toe of a slope
are difficult to defend if firefighters have to climb steep embankments.

## Additional Assessed Factors

Other physical features frequently cited as having great influence on the probability of
success or failure in structure-protection operations:

- Access roads and driveways: dead-ends, length, width, slope, grade, surface, turnarounds.
- Exterior construction: noncombustible, fire resistive, or combustible.
- Projections, overhangs, and stilt construction such as decks and eaves.
- Windows and other glazed openings: size, thickness, and protection.
- Vents and other openings into attics or foundations: presence or absence of screens.
- Fuel loading on land adjoining defensible space: type and amount of vegetation.
- Fuel stored within the defensible space, such as firewood and LPG.
- Aboveground power lines crossing over structures or defensible space.

## Assessment Under Field Conditions

Fire officers doing triage may have to do so from access roads, in smoke conditions, and
sometimes in darkness. Evaluation criteria must therefore be limited to those that can be
assessed quickly and easily under adverse conditions and from a distance.

Factors that can usually be judged rapidly and remotely: qualitative assessment of
defensible space; whether slope exceeds 20 percent; unfavourable siting such as the top
of a steep slope; projections such as balconies and decks; stilt construction; presence
of major power lines or service drops.

Factors that are difficult to assess from a distance and will often be unavailable:
window composition and protection, attic vents, exterior construction materials, roof
debris, fuel loading adjacent to defensible space, and onsite fuel storage such as LPG
tanks and firewood. Three-hundred-sixty-degree reconnaissance will probably be
infeasible due to time and distance limitations and the number of structures to be
evaluated.

Roof composition may be difficult to identify under such circumstances, but must be
assessed nevertheless because of its major importance to defensibility.

## Triage Decision Categories

Triage guidance is based on the number of compromising characteristics found at the
property:

- **Low count — probably survives without intervention.** Do not commit resources.
  Reassign them to structures where intervention changes the outcome.
- **Mid-range count — should be defended.** Commit resources; intervention is likely to
  be decisive.
- **Marginal cases requiring special attention to escape routes.** Defence may be
  attempted only with explicit, verified escape routes and safety zones, and with a
  trigger point for withdrawal established in advance.
- **High count — probably not salvageable even with intervention.** Write off. Do not
  commit crews.

An initial screening step precedes scoring, and can prohibit further triage or structure
protection outright. That screening covers access to the structure and its existing fire
condition.

## Limits of the Checklist Approach

Triage guidance is approximate. Officers using a triage checklist should temper the
decision to defend or not defend with judgement founded on experience. The true
probability of successfully defending a structure from wildfire is a matter of
considerable complexity and uncertainty, and even the best practical fire protection
methods have limited applicability to structure protection during wildland fires.

A checklist is most valuable precisely where officer experience is limited, and serves to
prompt the decision-maker operating in compressed timeframes without complete
fireground data.

## Evacuation Planning Components

Evacuation plan components should incorporate guidance on public notification, handling
transportation issues, addressing special-needs populations, evacuation of people's
animals, provision of good public information, evacuee tracking, relocation sites,
protection of evacuated areas, return of the population to evacuated areas, and
relocation and maintenance of continuity of government.
